Rules of engagement — the six ways this split goes wrong

1. E must not lose contact with users

The most common failure when a commercial co-founder owns discovery. Written summaries do not carry the information you need — watching a waiter's hands hesitate does. E attends ≥4 round-2 sessions and the entire pilot week, and reads round-1 transcripts rather than C's summaries of them.

2. C must not sell what does not exist

No delivery dates promised before certification is submitted. No feature described as existing unless it is on the tablet in C's hand at that moment. A missed date costs you the reference, and the reference is the point of the pilot.

3. The prototype must stay throwaway

C will want to show it to everyone, which is mostly good. Open every showing with "ovo je prototip, pola ovoga je lažno." Keep the dated delete note in the README. It goes in the bin after week 6; only @tr/domain survives.

4. Interview quality must not drift

Round 1 is script-driven and recorded. Round 2 uses the same task battery in the same order with a stopwatch, so twelve sessions produce comparable numbers rather than twelve impressions.

5. E's calendar is protected

Everything except the prototype, the fiscal adapter, the spine, four interviews and the pilot week routes to C. If E is chasing a government email, the split has failed.

6. Settle roles and equity before you take money

Two founders — technical plus commercial — in a regulated vertical is a materially easier seed story than one. But only if the split is documented. Do it while it is still easy.
